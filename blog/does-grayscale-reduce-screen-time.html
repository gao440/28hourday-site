Does grayscale actually reduce screen time? What the studies found

By Charles, developer of 28 Hour Day · July 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Turning your phone's screen gray sounds like the kind of productivity tip that works for exactly three days. No notifications blocked, no apps locked — the feed is all still there, just… beige. Could that possibly matter?

It's one of the few phone-habit tricks that's actually been tested in controlled studies. Here's what they found — including the one that found much less than the others, because you deserve the whole picture.

Why grayscale should work, in theory

Apps are colored on purpose. Notification badges are red because red pulls the eye. Food photos, like buttons, story rings — saturation is part of the reward. Color isn't why you open Instagram, but it's part of why Instagram is hard to put down: a vivid feed promises more than a gray one. Strip the color and the promise gets quieter. That's the theory: grayscale doesn't block the slot machine, it just turns off the lights on it.

Study 1: ~38 minutes less screen time per day

The most-cited test is Holte & Ferraro (2020, The Social Science Journal). College students who switched their phones to grayscale for several days cut their daily screen time by roughly 38 minutes per day compared to controls, with social media taking a meaningful share of the drop. Participants described their phones as simply less appealing — they still checked them, but put them down sooner.

Thirty-eight minutes is not a rounding error. It's almost exactly the average nightly in-bed scroll — the study's whole effect size is one bedtime doomscroll, every day.

Study 2: less anxiety, less problematic use

A follow-up by Holte and colleagues (2021, Current Psychology) found a week of grayscale reduced not just usage but anxiety scores and problematic-smartphone-use scores. A phone that's less rewarding tugs less at the back of your mind even when it's in your pocket.

Study 3: the one that disagrees

Honesty time. Dekker & Baumgartner (2024, Mobile Media & Communication) ran a preregistered test and found weaker, more mixed effects — some reduction in use for some participants, but not the clean 38-minute drop, and not for everyone. A fair reading of all three studies: grayscale reliably makes phones feel less appealing, often reduces use meaningfully, but is not a guaranteed fix on its own — especially for habits driven by boredom or compulsion rather than visual pull.

That matches what users consistently report: grayscale is a dampener, not a wall. Which suggests the right way to use it.

The right way to use it: scheduled, at night, alongside a real block

Most people who try grayscale set it all day, hate reading maps and photos in gray, and turn it off by Thursday. The smarter deployment is scheduled: full color all day, gray at your wind-down time. You lose nothing (nobody needs vivid TikTok at 11 PM) and you aim the intervention at the exact hours it helps. Gray also pairs naturally with a hard block: grayscale makes the feed easier to put down at 10 PM, and a real app block catches whatever willpower the gray screen doesn't.

How to schedule grayscale on iPhone (free, built-in)

  1. Set up the filter: Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters → Grayscale (leave the toggle off for now).
  2. Make it one tap: Settings → Accessibility → Accessibility Shortcut → Color Filters. Now triple-clicking the side button toggles gray.
  3. Automate it: in the Shortcuts app, create a Personal Automation — "At 10:00 PM → Set Color Filters On," and a second one at your wake time to turn it off. Set both to run immediately without asking.

That's the exact mechanic we built into 28 Hour Day as a one-tap setup: the screen loses its color at bedtime and gets it back in the morning, automatically, alongside the app block. The gray does the persuading; Screen Time does the enforcing.

Verdict

Does grayscale reduce screen time? Probably, and sometimes a lot — the best study says ~38 minutes a day, the follow-up adds lower anxiety, and the skeptical study still found it makes phones less appealing, just less dramatically. It costs nothing, takes five minutes to automate, and stacks with everything else. As a single fix it's a coin flip; as the soft half of a soft-plus-hard bedtime setup, it's one of the best-value habits changes on the phone.
