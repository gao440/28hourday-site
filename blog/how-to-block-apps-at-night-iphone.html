How to block apps at night on iPhone (so you actually can't open them)

By Charles, developer of 28 Hour Day · July 20, 2026 · 7 min read

If you've ever set up Screen Time with the best intentions and then tapped "Ignore Limit" three nights later without even thinking about it, this guide is for you.

The iPhone ships with three built-in ways to restrict apps at night — Downtime, App Limits, and Focus modes. All three are worth knowing. All three also share the same weakness: they were designed to be easy to override, and the person overriding them is you at 2 AM, half-asleep, one thumb already on Instagram. Here's how each one works, where it breaks, and what to do when the built-in tools aren't enough.

Option 1: Downtime (Settings → Screen Time)

Downtime is Apple's "phone goes to bed" feature. During the hours you set, only apps you've explicitly allowed stay available; everything else gets dimmed on your home screen.

  1. Open Settings → Screen Time → Downtime.
  2. Turn on Scheduled, then set your window — say, 10:30 PM to 7:00 AM. You can set different schedules per day.
  3. Under Screen Time → Always Allowed, choose the apps that should keep working (Phone, Messages, Maps, alarm apps).

The loophole: when Downtime kicks in, blocked apps show a "Time Limit" screen with an Ignore Limit button offering "One more minute," "Remind me in 15 minutes," or "Ignore limit for today." Unless you've set a Screen Time passcode, ignoring the block is literally one tap — less effort than unlocking your phone. And even with a passcode, most people set one they know, which means the barrier is typing four digits you've typed a hundred times.

Option 2: App Limits

App Limits caps total daily usage per app or category (for example, one hour of social media per day) rather than blocking by time of night. Set them under Settings → Screen Time → App Limits.

The loophole: the same Ignore Limit button — plus a quirk that matters at night: App Limits reset at midnight. If your problem is scrolling from 11 PM to 1 AM, a daily limit can hand you a fresh allowance right in the middle of the danger zone.

Option 3: A Sleep Focus

Focus modes (Settings → Focus → Sleep) silence notifications, hide distracting home-screen pages, and can pair with a sleep schedule in the Health app. This genuinely helps — a phone that isn't buzzing is a phone you pick up less.

The loophole: Focus filters what reaches you; it doesn't stop what you reach for. Nothing about a Sleep Focus prevents you from opening TikTok. If your pattern is notification-driven, Focus may be all you need. If your pattern is "I open the app myself, in the dark, on autopilot," Focus won't touch it.

Why the built-in tools fail at 2 AM specifically

None of this is a design flaw, exactly. Apple built Screen Time's limits to be advisory — a speed bump, not a wall — because for a general-purpose feature that's the safe choice. But bedtime is the worst possible moment for an advisory system. Late at night, the part of you that set the limit is asleep, and the part of you holding the phone just wants one more video. Research on people who delay sleep this way (there's a name for it: revenge bedtime procrastination) consistently finds the problem isn't knowledge or intention — it's that self-control is at its daily low exactly when the phone is most available.

A one-tap escape hatch, offered every single night to a tired brain, gets used. The numbers on bedtime phone use bear this out: half of adults use a screen in bed every single night, and most of them wish they didn't.

What actually works: make the block real, keep one honest exit

The fix isn't more willpower; it's moving the decision to the evening before, when you're still the version of you who wants to sleep. In practice that means a blocker with three properties:

  • It's enforced by the system, not by a reminder. Third-party blockers built on Apple's Screen Time framework (the same one parental controls use) can shield apps so they genuinely won't open — no Ignore Limit button.
  • It runs on a schedule you set once. If you have to arm it every night, you'll stop arming it. The block should begin at your bedtime automatically, every night.
  • It has a deliberate, limited escape hatch. Real life has exceptions — a sick kid, a friend in crisis. A blocker with no exit gets deleted within a week. The difference between a good escape hatch and Ignore Limit is friction and framing: a pause, your own words back to you, and an unlock that re-locks itself after 30 minutes instead of surrendering the whole night.

You can assemble some of this from Apple's parts — a Screen Time passcode you ask someone else to set, plus Downtime, gets close — but it's brittle, and one "for today" tap undoes it. This gap is exactly why we built 28 Hour Day, a bedtime app blocker for iPhone: you pick the apps and the bedtime once, Apple's Screen Time system enforces the window every night, and the only exit is a 30-minute unlock that re-locks itself.

A setup that works tonight, with or without an app

  1. Pick your three killer apps. Not everything — just the two or three that actually eat your nights. Blocking twenty apps feels thorough; blocking the right three works.
  2. Set the block to start 30–45 minutes before you want to be asleep. The wind-down is where nights are won.
  3. Add friction to whatever escape hatch remains. Ask a partner to set your Screen Time passcode, or use a blocker whose unlock makes you pause on purpose.
  4. Consider going gray at night. A colorless feed is dramatically less sticky — the grayscale research is genuinely encouraging.
